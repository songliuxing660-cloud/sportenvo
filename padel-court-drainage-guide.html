Padel Court Drainage: How to Keep an Outdoor Court Playable After Rain

Quick answer: a court that stays wet is usually showing a system problem, not just a turf problem. The playing surface, porous or graded base, perimeter drainage, ring-beam outlets, site outfall and any roof runoff must work together.

Why Wet Courts Become a Commercial Problem

Players in rainy markets repeatedly report courts that remain unavailable long after the weather clears. Wet turf changes traction, wet glass changes the rebound and saturated infill can make balls feel heavy. For an operator, every delayed reopening removes bookable hours and weakens trust in the venue.

Fast drying is not created by one product. It comes from a continuous path that takes water from the surface to a legal, adequate outfall.

Follow the water.

Start at the turf, continue through the base and perimeter drain, pass through or around the enclosure foundation, and finish at the approved outfall. One blocked interface can hold water inside the court.

Porous and Non-Porous Court Bases

Outdoor padel courts are commonly designed with a porous construction so water percolates through the turf, asphalt and aggregate base. Where a non-porous construction is used, the finished drainage strategy must use a suitable designed fall and collection route.

The 2025 SAPCA Code of Practice published through the LTA notes that new-build padel courts may be formed level at the playing surface with a 1:100 gradient at formation level where required to guide percolated water toward the perimeter drainage outfall. That is a UK technical reference, not a universal detail to copy without site engineering.

Soil infiltration, rainfall intensity, groundwater, frost, local drainage rules and the available outfall all change the design. The foundation must also satisfy the structural requirements of the court and any roof.

What Can Make Turf Drain Slowly?

Incorrect or contaminated infill

Fine material, dirt, organic debris and compacted infill can reduce drainage paths. More sand is not automatically better. Follow the turf system specification and maintain the surface with suitable equipment.

Blocked surface openings

Leaves, dust, moss and debris accumulate fastest at edges and low-use zones. A maintenance plan should include cleaning, brushing and inspection without damaging the fibres or pushing contamination deeper into the system.

Low spots below the turf

If the base is outside tolerance, water can remain in local depressions even when the rest of the court performs well. This needs a level survey, not a guess based on the visible carpet.

Covered Courts Still Need Drainage

A canopy changes the water problem rather than eliminating it. A large roof collects rainfall and delivers it to gutters and downpipes. If that flow discharges beside the ring beam or into an undersized system, the covered court can still flood from the perimeter.

  • Calculate roof catchment and design rainfall.
  • Keep downpipe discharge away from the playing-base build-up.
  • Coordinate roof and court drainage outfalls.
  • Allow for wind-driven rain through open sides.
  • Provide access for gutter, drain and outlet maintenance.

For canopy interfaces, see the padel court roof construction guide.

Diagnosing a Court That Stays Wet

  1. Record where water remains and how long each area takes to dry.
  2. Check whether the problem is surface-wide or local.
  3. Inspect turf contamination, infill condition and edge build-up.
  4. Survey surface and formation levels where information is available.
  5. Trace drains, ring-beam penetrations and the final outfall.
  6. Observe the system during rain, not only after it stops.
  7. Check for roof or adjacent hardscape water entering the court.
  8. Have the designer or civil contractor identify the cause before selecting a repair.

Drainage Questions to Ask Before Ordering

  • Is the proposed base porous or non-porous?
  • What is the site-specific level and drainage design?
  • Where is the perimeter drain and how is it accessed?
  • How does water cross the enclosure foundation?
  • Where is the legal outfall and what capacity is available?
  • How will roof water be collected if a canopy is added later?
  • Who is responsible for civil tolerances and drainage testing?
  • What maintenance keeps the surface and drains functioning?

Technical reference: LTA / SAPCA Code of Practice for padel courts. Use the current local standard and a qualified civil or structural designer for the project location.

Padel Court Drainage FAQ

Why does a padel court stay wet after rain?

Common causes include a non-porous or blocked base, inadequate perimeter drainage, poor outfall, unsuitable gradients, clogged turf drainage paths, excessive or incorrect infill, and roof runoff entering the court area.

Should an outdoor padel court have a porous base?

A porous construction is commonly used so water can pass through the turf and base to a drainage system. The correct design depends on soil, rainfall, local rules and the intended outfall.

Can a roof solve drainage problems?

A roof reduces direct rainfall on the playing surface but creates concentrated runoff. Gutters, downpipes and outfalls must be coordinated with the court drainage.

Can drainage be fixed after installation?

Some maintenance problems can be corrected, but defects in levels, base construction, ring-beam outlets or outfall capacity can require disruptive civil work. Investigate the cause before choosing a repair.
