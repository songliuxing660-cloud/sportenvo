Padel Court Lighting: Lux, LED, Uniformity and Glare

Quick answer: good padel lighting is not the brightest system. It gives players consistent visibility of the ball and glass without placing harsh sources in the upward sightlines used for lobs, bandejas and smashes.

Why Players Notice Padel Lighting So Quickly

A current Reddit discussion about ceiling LEDs versus traditional projectors repeats a long-running complaint: one bright corner can make an overhead ball disappear. Padel creates a difficult lighting task because players look up frequently, follow a fast ball against different backgrounds and use transparent walls that reflect bright sources.

That feedback is a design signal, not a substitute for a photometric calculation. The solution begins with the playing task and the building geometry.

Design for visibility, not fixture count.

Lux, uniformity, glare control, vertical light, contrast and spill must be assessed together. A high average lux value can still produce a poor court.

The Lighting Metrics That Matter

MetricWhy it matters on a padel court
Horizontal illuminanceLight measured on the playing plane; useful for overall court brightness
UniformityControls the difference between bright and dark zones as the ball travels
Vertical illuminanceHelps players, spectators and cameras see the ball and people above the floor plane
Glare rating and shieldingLimits discomfort and lost visibility when players look toward a fitting
Colour renderingSupports clear perception of the ball, lines, turf and players
Flicker performanceImportant for visual comfort and high-frame-rate video
Spill light and candelaControls impact on nearby homes, roads and the night environment

How Many Lux Does a Padel Court Need?

There is no single number for every project. Recreational play, competition and television broadcast have different requirements. The current FIP tour rulebook requires at least 1000 lux of vertical illumination for television broadcasts, while venue and national guidance may specify different values for community or competition courts.

An LTA padel guidance example has used 300 lux with 0.7 uniformity and a 0.9 maintenance factor for a court-lighting design. Treat that as a published reference point, not a universal specification. The project brief should identify the intended level of play, governing standard, measurement grid, vertical requirements and maintained performance before a supplier sizes the system.

Why Bright LEDs Can Still Feel Bad

Low mounting position

A fitting near the court frame can fall directly behind a high ball from the player's viewpoint. Mounting height and aiming can matter as much as raw output.

Visible high-intensity sources

LEDs are efficient, but a visible cluster of intense diodes can create sharp discomfort if the optic and shielding are wrong for sport.

Poor uniformity

A ball moving from a dark roof zone into a bright beam appears to change visibility. Players may describe this as the ball disappearing even when the average lux figure is acceptable.

Reflections from glass

Transparent walls can reflect fittings and multiply distracting bright points. The calculation and visual review should include the glass, not only the floor.

Side Projectors vs Overhead Lighting

SystemPotential advantageDesign risk
Court-frame floodlightsCommon, compact and compatible with many outdoor installationsLow visible sources, corner glare and spill if optics are generic
Higher external columnsMore aiming distance and potential separation from player sightlinesLarger planning footprint, wind loads and off-site light control
Ceiling LED lines or arraysCan distribute light across an indoor court without frame-mounted projectorsRequires enough clear height, good diffusion, service access and roof coordination
Hybrid systemCan address both horizontal and vertical targetsMore commissioning complexity and possible conflicting glare directions

No layout is automatically superior. Ask for a court-specific calculation and fixture aiming schedule rather than selecting by product photograph.

Covered Courts Need Daylight Analysis Too

Open-sided canopies can create a bright exterior beside a dark interior. At certain sun angles, the ball crosses that contrast rapidly. Roof shadows can also divide one court into visibly different zones.

  • Study morning and evening sun orientation.
  • Model daylight through each open side.
  • Consider adjustable screening where low sun is predictable.
  • Coordinate artificial lighting for overcast and twilight conditions.
  • Avoid placing opaque structural members behind important ball paths where possible.

For roof geometry and clearance, use the padel court roof guide.

Lighting Design and Commissioning Checklist

  1. Define recreational, competition or broadcast use.
  2. Confirm the governing local and federation requirements.
  3. Provide court, roof, column, glass and neighbouring-property geometry.
  4. Request horizontal and vertical calculations, uniformity and glare assessment.
  5. Review fixture locations from real player sightlines.
  6. Check spill light, curfew and planning conditions.
  7. Coordinate cabling, switching, controls and maintenance access.
  8. Measure the completed installation and correct the aiming before opening.

Technical references: FIP Rules and competition documents and LTA / SAPCA padel court guidance. Use the current project-specific standard.

Padel Court Lighting FAQ

How many lux does a padel court need?

The required illuminance depends on recreational, competition or broadcast use and on the governing standard. Lux alone is not enough: uniformity, glare, vertical illumination, spill light and maintenance factor must be designed and verified together.

Why do padel court lights cause glare?

Players repeatedly look upward to track lobs and overhead shots. Low-mounted or poorly aimed high-output fittings can sit directly in that field of view, while bright-dark contrast and visible LED sources can make the effect worse.

Are ceiling LED strips better than side floodlights?

A well-designed overhead system can reduce some direct glare, but the result depends on mounting height, optics, spacing, output and roof geometry. Neither layout is automatically glare-free.

Should lighting be tested after installation?

Yes. Commissioning measurements should confirm the agreed horizontal and vertical illuminance, uniformity and aiming, and the system should be checked from real player sightlines.
